**Takeaway:** The EU's new Product Liability Directive treats software, including AI systems, as a product. It covers products placed on the market from 9 December 2026. If you sell AI-enabled gadgets or apps, a defect can now lead to a damages claim that needs no proof of fault. Your role in the supply chain decides how exposed you are.

What changes

- The new rules apply to products placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026 (Directive (EU) 2024/2853, Art. 2(1)). Member States must have national laws in place by the same date (Art. 22(1)).

- "Product" now includes software (Art. 4(1)). Recital 13 names AI systems. It also says AI Act providers should be treated as manufacturers.

- Liability does not depend on fault. The claimant must prove the defect, the damage and the link between them (Art. 10(1)).

Who pays

- The manufacturer is liable first (Art. 8(1)(a)).

- If the manufacturer is outside the EU, liability also falls on the importer and the authorised representative. If there is neither, it falls on the fulfilment service provider (Art. 8(1)(c)).

- If you sell under your own brand, you count as the manufacturer (Art. 4(10)(b)).

- If you substantially modify a product outside the maker's control and then sell it, you become its manufacturer (Art. 8(2)).

- A plain distributor is liable in one case only. An injured person asks you to name an EU operator or your own supplier, and you fail to do so within one month (Art. 8(3)). Online marketplaces face the same rule where they look like the seller under DSA Art. 6(3) (Art. 8(4)).

Why AI matters here

- The defect test looks at a product's ability to keep learning after sale (Art. 7(2)(c)). It also looks at safety-relevant cybersecurity requirements (Art. 7(2)(f)).

- A defect is presumed if the product breaks mandatory EU or national safety rules meant to prevent that damage (Art. 10(2)(b)).

- Courts can order you to disclose evidence (Art. 9(1)). If you don't, a defect is presumed (Art. 10(2)(a)).

- Covered damage includes death or personal injury, including recognised harm to mental health. It also covers damage to private property and the loss or corruption of non-professional data (Art. 6(1)).

Best practice

- Match each AI-enabled product you sell to a named EU manufacturer, importer or authorised representative.

- Keep supplier records you can produce within one month.

- Review your own-brand lines. For those, you are the manufacturer.

- Get update and cybersecurity commitments from suppliers in writing.

This sits beside the AI Act, not inside it. Your AI Act distributor and importer duties (Arts. 23–25) still apply.
