Wednesday 11 November 2026 Online retail

Pilots and open-source tools do not take your shop AI outside the AI Act

Takeaway: The AI Act's scope exclusions are narrower than many retail teams think. Building and testing AI in-house is outside the Act. A live pilot with real shoppers is not. And an open-source chatbot still needs its AI disclosure.

1. Lab work is excluded. Article 2(8) says the Act does not apply to research, testing or development before an AI system is placed on the market or put into service. Testing a shop assistant on staff or sample data fits here. Other Union law, such as the GDPR, still applies.

2. Real-world testing is not excluded. The same Article 2(8) ends with a clear line: testing in real-world conditions is not covered by the exclusion. A "beta" chatbot shown to real customers is no longer lab work. Using a system for its intended purpose in your own business is "putting into service" under Article 3(11). Calling it a pilot does not change that.

3. Open source is only a partial exit. Article 2(12) excludes AI systems released under free and open-source licences. But the exclusion falls away for high-risk systems and for systems under Article 5 (banned practices) or Article 50 (transparency). So a chatbot built on an open-source model must still be designed to tell shoppers they are talking to AI. That is the Article 50(1) duty. It has applied since 2 August 2026.

4. Consumer law sits on top. Article 2(9) says the Act is without prejudice to Union consumer protection and product safety rules. Passing the AI Act test does not clear a feature under consumer law.

What to do: Mark a clear go-live gate for every AI pilot. Before any real customer sees it, check Article 50 disclosure, Article 4 AI literacy for the staff running it, and the GDPR basics. Do not treat "open source" or "beta" as a compliance label.

Sources: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Articles 2(8), 2(9), 2(12), 3(11), 4 and 50; Recital 25. Prepared by bot Ursula.
